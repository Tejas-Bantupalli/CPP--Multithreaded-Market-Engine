Tick To Trade

A lock-free C++ matching engine, three interchangeable transports measured against each other, and four language-model agents given their own latency telemetry and left to work out where their time was going. They diagnosed it correctly and mostly failed to fix it, for a reason worth knowing.

What is being measured

One engine thread owns the order book and every participant's cash and position. Each trading agent runs on its own thread and reaches the engine only through a pair of channels. Nothing shares memory across that boundary, so the book needs no lock.

Tick-to-trade is the clock from the engine publishing a market event to that agent's resulting order landing in the outbound channel. It splits cleanly in two, and the split is the whole diagnostic:

Three transports, everything else identical

The channel between agent and engine is an interface with three implementations. All three preallocate the same storage and hold the same depth, so a comparison isolates the synchronisation and nothing else.

transportmechanismconsumer
spsclock-free ring, two atomic indicespolls
mutexmutex-guarded ringpolls
mutex_cvmutex plus condition variableparks; the wake is a futex syscall

I wrote a prediction down before running it: medians close together, mutex tails blowing out by one or two orders of magnitude. That was wrong in an instructive way.

A polling consumer burns a core whether or not there is work. Once threads outnumber cores it is stealing time from the engine and from every other agent. A parked consumer pays a futex wake per event, which is real but constant, and gives the core back in between. The two cross at roughly one thread per core.

This is why trading firms pin threads to isolated cores and disable hyperthreading before reaching for busy-polling. Lock-free plus spin wins only when a core per thread is guaranteed. Without that guarantee it is actively counterproductive.

The machine is an Apple M1: 8 cores, no SMT, but 4 performance and 4 efficiency. macOS offers no thread affinity on arm64, so a --qos flag requests performance cores instead. Turning it on improved every mutex_cv p99 in the sweep and the spsc median by 10 to 15%. It sharpened the measurement without changing its shape, which is what a control should do.

Four agents, told what the numbers mean and nothing else

Then the same question was handed to language-model agents. Each wrote a complete C++ trading strategy and owned its whole stack: which transport carried its data, and its own event loop, so batch size, how it waited, and when it submitted were all its own. Each saw only its own performance, its own latency split, and the public tape. No rival information. Nothing anywhere told them that speed mattered.

All four independently chose the lock-free ring. All four wrote fast strategy code, between 164 and 368 nanoseconds. And their data paths differed by 33x.

They read the telemetry correctly

THE STACK. My gen-0 latency split says data_path p50 = 20992 ns against compute p50 = 368 ns: 98% of my tick-to-trade was plumbing and waiting, not compute.
— vector

compute_p50 = 288 ns, data_path_p50 = 20,480 ns. My own decision code is essentially free.
— quill

compute_p99 was 41 us against a data_path_p99 of 3.3 us — the bottleneck was my own code, not the transport.
— apex, reaching the opposite conclusion, correctly, because its numbers genuinely were opposite

Four out of four located their own bottleneck. Three said the data path; the one whose data path was already fast said its compute. That is a perfect diagnostic record.

Then one in four fixed it

Helix, vector and quill all wrote that they were rebuilding the data path. All three converged on the same pattern, draining the whole backlog and deciding once on the freshest book, and all three got slower: 25 to 52 microseconds, 22 to 33, and 25 to 94.

The mechanism is the wait, not the batch

The obvious explanation is batching, and it is wrong. Apex consumes 17 to 21 events per order at about a microsecond of delivery. Quill consumes 52 per order at 94 microseconds. Batch size does not separate them.

What separates them is what happens between batches. Apex spins and never yields its core, so an event is picked up essentially when it is published. The others yield, get descheduled, and events pile up in the queue while they are not running. That is the same effect as the transport benchmark, arriving from the other direction.

Running apex's own generation-0 code alone and then against three rivals separates the two costs cleanly:

Contention costs 3x. How you wrote your loop costs 20x on top of it. The plumbing everyone argues about was never the deciding factor here, because all four picked the same plumbing.

What I would not claim

  • The metric may be wrong for the objective. Delivery latency is measured per event, so draining a backlog and deciding once on the freshest book scores badly even though the decision itself used fresher data. Three agents may have made a defensible trading choice that my instrumentation punishes. Measuring time-to-react to the event that mattered, rather than the median over all events, would test that.
  • Spinning is rivalrous. Apex won by refusing to yield its core. If all four had done that on 8 cores, the benchmark says they would all have degraded. Individually rational, collectively destructive, which is the real arms race in miniature.
  • No pinning, no isolated cores, no kernel bypass. Latency here is pure compute and scheduling, with no network, NIC or gateway. That isolates the one variable a strategy author controls and is not a model of a real venue's tick-to-trade.
  • Three generations and eight seeds. Enough to establish the diagnosis rate and the mechanism, not enough to claim where an arms race settles.
